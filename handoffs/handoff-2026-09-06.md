### Final state (tool-verified via `span.mjs` at commit 9a9f9ad)

Three single-note lines. No block chords anywhere — harmony is implied
across time.

**pulse1 (melody, `sf-violin`), G4–C6.** Per 4-bar phrase: a (bar 1),
b (bar 2), a′ (bar 3), c (bar 4). Bar 4 lands C5 by step; bar 8 lands
**C6** via a leap through B5. Same pitch class, very different weight.

**pulse2 (counterline, `square25`), G♯3–G♯4.** First pass as written,
then **climbs an octave**, pivoting on the last note of bar 6
(F3 → F4) rather than at the phrase boundary. Bars 7–8 sit an octave
above 3–4 — with one deliberate exception, bar 7 beat 3 (see below).

**Late change — bar 7 beat 3, F4 → A♯4.** Made after Josh had already
declared the song finished, and it is a real improvement on two axes:

- *Counterpoint.* The octave transposition had put pulse2's F4 directly
  under the melody's F5 — a bare octave between two supposedly
  independent lines, at the busiest point in the piece. Bar 3 has the
  same pitches two octaves apart, where it is inaudible as doubling; at
  one octave the voices collapse into one. A♯4 makes it a perfect fifth
  and the lines stay separate.
- *Variation.* Bar 3 is G♯–F–G♯, a lower neighbor. Bar 7 is now
  G♯–A♯–G♯, an **upper** neighbor. Same rhythm, same departure and return
  note, direction inverted — so bars 3 and 7 became a′ and a″ instead of
  the same bar at two octaves.

Costs nothing chromatically (A♯ was already in the bar 2/6 melody, and B♭
is diatonic to C natural minor). Census unchanged. Creates the piece's
only quartal sonority: bar 7 beat 3 is C3 / A♯4 / F5 — C, F, B♭ stacked
in fourths — landing on the one beat where the consequent departs from
the antecedent.

**How Josh found it: visually, not audibly.** He was looking at the
pulse2 lane and saw the contour dip before its descent; moving the note
up made "more of a mountain shape." He wasn't sure it would work
musically. It does. From bar 6 the line now reads G3 → D4 → E♭4 → F4 →
G♯4 → **A♯4** → G♯4 → G4 → F4 → E♭4: a single summit and a stepwise
descent, where before there were two humps. Bonus he wasn't aiming for —
pulse2 peaks at bar 7 beat 3 and the melody peaks at bar 8 beat 3, so the
two climaxes are **staggered by a bar** rather than simultaneous.

Worth recording that Josh and the tutor reached the same fix from
different evidence — he saw a broken contour, the tutor heard an octave
doubling. Note also that a piano roll represents pitch contour faithfully
but misrepresents harmonic weight (every semitone drawn the same size)
and shows no metric stress, so eye-composing is trustworthy for contour
specifically and not in general.

**triangle (bass, `sf-cello`), B2–E♭3.** Bars 5–8 identical to 1–4 — a
4-bar ostinato under an 8-bar period. Ends bar 8 beat 4 on an E♭3–D3
walking pickup that hands back to bar 1.

**Late change — the bass gained a pulse.** Bars 1/3/5/7 previously held
E♭3 for two beats; they now strike it twice as quarters. Purely
rhythmic — not one pitch changed. Josh: it *"gives the bass a pulse that
it didn't have, a timekeeping job."* Correct diagnosis: the line had
pitch motion but only two attacks per bar, which is a shape rather than a
beat. Re-striking is also deeply idiomatic for the NES triangle channel,
which has no envelope, so sustained notes drone and chip writers
re-articulate constantly.

**Bars 2 and 6 deliberately kept their held B2.** His first pass halved
every bar, which erased something: bars 4 and 8 had *already* struck B2
twice since the C-section rewrite, in sync with pulse2's chord change on
beat 2 — the bass participating in the cadential compression. Making all
eight bars uniform removed that marker and flattened the rhythmic
hierarchy (bass in halves / pulse2 in quarters / melody in quarters plus
subdivisions) down to two tiers. Restoring the held B2 at bars 2 and 6
only keeps the pulse in six bars and gives the cadence its contrast back.
Final state does this.

Note for anyone reading the history: Josh recalled that bars 4 and 8 had
*not* been halved before. The file at 5d96a31 shows they had. He accepted
the correction.

Pitch-class census: C D D♯ F G G♯ A♯ B. **Absent: C♯ E F♯ A.**

### Key vertical moments

- **Bars 4 and 8, beat 1** — melody E♭ over pulse2's G over the
  triangle's B: **G augmented**. In the earlier homophonic version the D
  inside the block chord made this a clash; removing the chords turned
  the same note into a sonority.
- **Bar 4 beat 2** — D over F over B: full **B diminished** (vii° of Cm).
- **Bar 8 beat 2** — B over F over B: a **bare tritone**, nothing else,
  one beat before the arrival. So the antecedent gets the padded version
  of the cadence and the consequent the naked one.
- **Bar 2 beat 1** — melody A♭ over G and B. The song's signature
  dissonance, now fully exposed with only three voices.

### Form

Per 4-bar phrase a/b/a′/c; two phrases make a **period** (weak cadence
then strong). Josh named the a-b-a-c shape himself. Because it loops,
the bass runs as a continuous 4-bar ostinato underneath.

## 2. Derivation path — how it got here (the non-circular record)

1. Josh brought a progression found on his piano: A♭, Fm, G — three
   chords, no resolution. The tutor suggested trying Cm at the end (a
   rule-1 breach; see §8). Josh tried it, kept it, and reported it
   unblocked him: *"once I got that C minor, I had all sorts of
   inspiration, and I worked for a couple of hours."* This is the one
   place in either session where a tutor suggestion entered the music.
   He then built it out to 2 bars, then 4 by adding bars 3–4, describing
   the melody as A B A′ C.
2. Tutor observed the material was actually A B A′ **B′** — bar 4 was
   bar 2 up an octave, same notes, rhythm and held G. Josh: *"You're
   right. I didn't really have a c, so I changed it."* Rewrote bar 4 with
   new contour landing on C — the first cadential arrival on C in the piece.
3. Tutor flagged: if bars 3–4 move to make room for new middle material,
   protect that arrival staying last. Josh expanded to 8 bars and **it did**
   — softer stepwise landing at bar 4, the leap kept at bar 8.
4. Josh found the second dissonance himself. Tutor had named only the
   A♭-over-G; Josh raised the B-over-Fm unprompted and then stated the
   generalization: in both cases the non-chord tone is in the **melody**
   and the chords stay clean. His finding, recorded as his.
5. Instrumentation: violin / drawbar organ / cello. Melody topped at C7 —
   tutor flagged that as the top of the violin's practical range, where a
   soundfont goes thin. Josh confirmed by ear (*"that c seven is a little
   bit strained"*), considered a whole-step transposition, asked whether
   the cello would survive it. Answer: yes comfortably (A2 vs the cello's
   C2 floor), but a whole step buys relief, not a register. He **chose the
   octave instead** — melody and chords down one, cello left alone.
6. Overnight he made the decision to go **monophonic**. Rewrote pulse2
   from block triads into a single counterline.
7. Josh then soloed all three **pairs** of voices, unprompted, and
   reported every combination held up — which it had not in the
   homophonic version. He arrived at the standard test for contrapuntal
   writing by ear.
8. Josh's own diagnosis of why he made the octave climb: *"that middle
   part wasn't a line on its own. It wasn't a good melody on its own…
   this needs something else to be a real line."* He then applied the same
   test to the bass, heard repetition rather than filler, and **decided it
   was fine**. Both calls independently correct — see §5.

**Attribution note.** Josh: *"you didn't really help with those decisions.
I made these decisions primarily on my own."* Accurate for everything from
step 2 onward. The one exception is the Cm in step 1, which Josh himself
raised later and credited. Other tutor contributions across both sessions:
reading note data back, catching that bar 4 was B′ not C, the violin-range
flag, and naming things after he built them (period, hierarchical phrase

## 5. Teaching observations worth keeping

**Voice-leading reduction, performed unaided and by ear.** Going
monophonic meant reducing block triads to a single line that still
implies the harmony — choosing, beat by beat, which chord tone carries
the harmony forward while leaving a singable line. Josh did this for
eight bars with no rule to follow and described it as *"so freaking fun."*

Bar 8 shows what the ear produced: from G–B–D he kept G, from F–B–D he
kept F, from the Cm he kept E♭. Each chord offered two other options.
That specific combination is the only one that yields a stepwise descent
(G–F–E♭) **and** the only one that moves against the melody. Every
alternative would have leapt, doubled the melody, or moved with it.

**Contrary motion at the consequent cadence, contrasted against similar
motion at the antecedent.** Josh raised this himself — *"those two
instruments go in opposite directions, which I did more or less
intentionally."* Verified:

- Bar 8: melody E♭5 → B5 → C6 rising, pulse2 G4 → F4 → E♭4 falling. The
  gap opens from 8 to 21 semitones.
- Bar 4: melody E♭5 → D5 → C5 and pulse2 G3 → F3 → E♭3, **both falling**,
  spacing near-constant at 20–21 semitones.

So the two cadences differ on a second axis beyond landing note: similar
motion with fixed spacing versus contrary motion that diverges. The first
closes in on itself, the second opens out. Josh: *"I wouldn't say I
planned it, but I felt it."*

**Arpeggiation, reinvented from the constraint.** Josh described halving
a chord's duration to state two of its tones in sequence when one voice
couldn't hold both. That is arpeggiation, and it's the defining solution
of the NES idiom — two pulses and a triangle cannot state a full chord
and carry melody and bass, so chip writers arpeggiate. He arrived at it
because the constraint demanded it.

**His recalled rule for those splits, checked against the data.** Josh,
from memory: *"I want to emphasize the dissonance… I took the note that
produced just a semitone of dissonance first. And then the next note
would be more space."*

Verified at bar 2/6 beats 1–2 (the split of the old G–B–D): pulse2 G3
under melody A♭4 is 13 semitones, a **minor ninth** — the piece's
sharpest dissonance — then D4 under B♭4 is 8 semitones, a consonant minor
sixth. Exactly as he remembered, and it's the split that matters most.

Reversed at bar 2/6 beats 3–4: E♭4 under G4 is a consonant major third,
then F3 is a compound major second, the dissonant one — dissonance second.

Reading worth keeping: the actual practice is better than the rule he
remembered. Beat 1 is a downbeat, where a dissonance has weight and needs
resolution; beat 4 is a pickup, where a dissonance leans into the next
bar. He placed the clash where the meter wanted it in both cases, and
those happen to be opposite orderings.

**Harmonic minor is a catalog-wide habit, and it's measurable.** Josh
raised it himself: *"It seems like almost every song I do has a harmonic
minor idea in it."* Census across his own compositions confirms it:

| song | key | raised 7th |
|------|-----|-----------|
| graveyard | F♯m | E♯ (F♮) ×40 / 21.5q vs E♮ ×14 / 7.5q — **three times the duration** |
| graveyard-2 | F♯m | same profile |
| graveyard-3 | F♯m | same profile |
| test-sad-progression | Cm | B♮ is the **only** chromatic note in the piece |
| cool-bmaj-progression | G♯m | **absent** — G♮ never appears; pure natural minor |

Four of five, and the exception is conspicuous. Josh's attributed causes,
in his own order: Marty Friedman, Uematsu, and — raised after — *"I've
also practiced that scale into every arpeggio in the harmonic minor.
Chord progressions over and over."* The practice is likely the larger
cause; he wrote this progression *"too tired to figure it out myself,"*
which is exactly the condition where drilled material surfaces.

**He has riffs built over harmonic minor that have never been committed
to a song.** *"Some of them are quite good. I just haven't committed them
to any song yet."* Possible future work.

**Stated working preference: chords first, riffs occasionally.** Josh:
starting from a progression is *"slightly easier"* than writing a riff and
harmonizing it, and he wants to keep doing that, while treating riff-first
as *"a valuable thing to do from time to time."* Recorded as his method,
not a prescription.

**Correction to an earlier tutor framing.** The tutor described this piece
as having been written inside a fixed frame (8 bars, 3 voices, a stated
goal). Josh pushed back: the length wasn't planned, it grew 2 → 4 → 8, and
he invoked George R. R. Martin's gardener-versus-architect distinction —
*"I didn't have an outline here."* He's right; only the sad-song goal was

**Josh applied the pair-solo test twice and read it differently each
time** — correctly both times. On pulse2: *"wasn't a line on its own"* →
diagnosed as filler, fixed with the octave climb. On the triangle:
*"a little bit boring… but the intent is not to solo that track"* →
diagnosed as repetitive-but-structural, accepted. Distinguishing "this
voice is filler" from "this voice is dull alone but load-bearing" is

**He now hears problems before he can name them.** *"I have, like, sort
of more deeply nested a and b and c parts now… I don't know how to

**He derived the ♭VI / iv / V analysis unaided on the second day**, out

## 6. Glossary — PROPOSED additions

Both came up with anchors in Josh's own music.

- **period** — two phrases as antecedent + consequent, first ending
  weakly, second strongly. Anchor: test-sad-progression bars 1–8; bar 4
  lands C5 by step, bar 8 lands C6 by leap over the same harmony.
  Recommended status: **P-side found, R-side R2** — he built the shape
  deliberately and protected it when expanding, but did not have the term.
- **hierarchical phrase structure** — the same letter-labeling applied at
  multiple levels, so a letter denotes different-sized units by level.
  Josh raised it himself and couldn't name it. Anchor: bar-level a/b/a′/c
  inside 4-bar phrases inside the 8-bar period. Status: **encountered**.
- **voice-leading reduction** — reducing block chords to a single line
  that still implies the harmony. Anchor: the whole monophonic rewrite;
  bar 8's G–F–E♭ in particular. Recommended: **P-side high, R-side R1** —
  he executed it across eight bars unaided and enjoyed it, and had no
  term for it at all.
- **contrary motion / similar motion** — voices moving in opposite vs.
  the same direction. Anchor: bar 8 vs. bar 4 (see §5). He produced the
  contrast and named the effect (*"opposite directions"*) without the
  terms. Recommended: **P2** — he called it *"more or less
  intentional."*
- **arpeggiation** — spelling a chord horizontally. Anchor: his
  halved-duration splits. Note this is the central technique of the NES
  idiom he's writing in, which may make it worth a fuller treatment than
  a glossary line.
- **neighbor tone (upper/lower)** — anchor: bar 3's G♯–F–G♯ against bar
  7's G♯–A♯–G♯, which he created as a contour fix without naming it.
- **harmonic minor** — natural minor with a raised 7th, which is what
  turns the v into a functional V with a leading tone. Anchor:
  test-sad-progression's B♮, plus the F♯m raised 7th across the graveyard
  set (see §5). Recommended status: **long since used, never named** —
  Josh has been drilling the scale and its arpeggios for some time and
  derived the whole ♭VI/iv/V relationship himself, but reached for the
  term only when it was supplied. Whatever status the scale gets should
  reflect that this is retroactive labeling of existing practice, not new
  material.
- **(consider) the pair-solo test** — not a theory term, but he derived it
  independently and it's now part of his working method. May belong in the
  journal rather than the glossary.

`period` in particular is another instance of the "found it in my own
music but didn't have the word" case, which the existing binary R/P scale
has nowhere to put — same shape as compound melody. Further evidence for
the **two-axis R/P scale** already awaiting Josh's ruling in
`open-items.md`.

## 9. Level assessment — asked for, and answered against the record

Josh asks this regularly and is explicit about why: *"I'm not doing it to
compare myself against someone else. I'm doing it to compare myself to
myself."* Answer him honestly; the benchmark is worthless otherwise.

**The prior data point.** On 2026-08-26, on the key-change piece, the
assessment was *competent intermediate*, with three named things that
would move it up: counterpoint (real independent lines rather than
melody-plus-accompaniment), development (transforming a motif rather than
repeating it transposed), and proportion.

**Two of the three are now done.**

- *Counterpoint* — three independent lines, voice-leading reduction by
  ear, contrary motion at the cadence contrasted against similar motion,
  no parallel perfect intervals, staggered climaxes, and he invented the
  pair-solo test to verify his own work.
- *Development* — bar 8 vs bar 4, bar 7 vs bar 3, the octave ascent. The
  sharpest evidence is what he rejected: his first bar 4 was bar 2
  transposed up an octave, which is exactly the named failure mode, and
  he replaced it with real transformation within one turn of it being
  pointed out.
- *Proportion* — untested. Eight looping bars have no proportions to get
  wrong.

**The honest qualifier.** This piece is narrow: one progression, no
modulation, no B section, no key change — his own observation. The
2026-08-26 piece was harmonically more ambitious. He did not level up
uniformly; he traded harmonic reach for contrapuntal control, and the
trade paid.

**Current read.** Harmonic and contrapuntal craft is now solidly past
intermediate — clean undergraduate three-voice writing. **Rhythm is the
remaining beginner-level parameter**: quarters and halves throughout, one
triplet he's unsure of, one eighth-note pickup. Every other parameter has
developed and that one hasn't moved. **Scale is the untested axis** —
everything he has finished is short.

### Rhythm: the assessment does NOT apply to this song

Josh pushed back on whether this piece needs more rhythmic variety, and
he's right. Recorded so a future session doesn't "fix" a piece that isn't
broken:

- It loops, and rhythmic variety is what makes a loop seam audible.
- Its function is stillness; rhythmic activity reads as energy.
- It already has a rhythmic hierarchy — bass, pulse2, melody at three
  distinct rates. (Preserved through the late bass change: see §1. Bars
  2 and 6 keep held half notes specifically so the bass is not uniformly
  in quarters.)
- Rhythm is already used structurally in exactly one place: the harmonic
  rhythm doubles at bars 4 and 8, the cadence. One deliberate use at the
  one structural moment.

Josh's own framing: *"there are things that are moving at different paces
in this song, but never anything that throws you or that takes you out of
the song."* The skill being undeveloped and this song needing more rhythm
are separate claims; the first is true and the second isn't.

**CAUTION for future sessions.** After hearing the rhythm assessment Josh
said it made him *"slightly self conscious"* and he changed the piece.
The change turned out well and he kept it on the ear's verdict, but the
trigger was the assessment rather than a heard problem, and his first
pass overshot by erasing the cadence marker. A level assessment is not a
to-do list. When one is given, say plainly which parts do not apply to
the piece in hand — and if he starts editing right after receiving one,
check the motive before evaluating the edit.
