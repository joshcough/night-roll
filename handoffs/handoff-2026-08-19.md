## How it was derived — the reasoning path

Recorded because the path is the valuable part, not the chords.

**1. The closed-loop diagnosis (Josh's, from the previous session).** His A
parts loop so well they resist continuation — a perfect loop is a closed
system with no unresolved tension pointing outward. The strength and the
problem are the same property. Fix isn't "write a B part," it's "find where
the A part could stop closing." That located the work at the loop seam.

**2. The break already contained the exit.** Josh built a break (everything
drops out but bass and drums) that returns home, twice. Claude noted that a
feint which always resolves the same way stops being a feint — the third
time is when leaving does the most work. Josh then observed the break's bass
spells **C#–E–A**, an A major triad, and that **A♮ and G♮ are the only two
non-diatonic pitches in the entire song**.

**3. Josh's own reading of the two foreign notes, which was correct:** G♮
sits a half step below G# and functions as a leading tone — it exists to
deliver you home, so it's approach, not destination. **A♮ is the structural
one.** Claude supplied only the name: A major on ♭2 of G# minor is the
**Neapolitan**, and its standard use is exactly the pivot Josh proposed.

**4. Josh's architecture, arrived at before he had the theory:** first break
hints at the foreign element with no consequence; second break pays it off.
That's foreshadowing, deliberately structured.

**5. Making A the tonic rather than a color.** The answer was *time* (sit on
it, return to it, resolve phrases to it) plus *removing G♮* — the note that
drags you home. Josh removed it. The second break ends on A instead. Smallest
possible change, largest effect — same principle as the loop-seam
single-note idea.

**6. Modulation destination.** Josh reasoned A→C#m mirrors G#m→B inverted
(minor→major becomes major→minor). Then derived independently that **C#m is
iv of G# minor and iii of A major** — a genuine pivot chord, available as
the road home.

**7. Chromatic approach.** Josh noticed his section arrivals are all
approached by step from below (G♮→G#, B→C#). Diatonic transposition of
G#–A#–B gives A–B–C#, which arrives by whole step rather than half — softer.
He tried the chromatic lift anyway: bar 14.1 is **A2 → C3 → C#3**, a passing
sixteenth. Held long it would read as a key change; as a sixteenth it's
friction on the way in.

**8. Where to go after A6 → C#m.** Josh's gut said don't return to A. His
reasoning, which was right: in the A part the third chord is G#m7 — the
tonic *altered*, not a plain return. Enumerated triads sharing two pitches
with A (C#m, F#m; Am via modal mixture). Chose **E7** instead — dominant of
A resolving to **Am**, a deceptive flavour. Then **Am → G#m** moves all
three voices by half step (A→G#, C→B, E→D#), which is chromatic voice
leading and about the smoothest connection between two triads sharing
nothing.

**9. Harmonic rhythm as a lever.** Josh proposed compressing: F#m for a bar,
then E7 and Am at half a bar each. Everything prior changes chord at a fixed
rate, so speeding up into the return builds momentum. **He found one of the
five B-part contrast dimensions by hand, without having done the study.**

**10. The register problem — the real find.** Josh reported the ending felt
"lost on the return loop." Diagnosis: not harmony at all. The melody peaked
at **A6**; the loop target at 2.4 puts pulse1 on **B5**. A major-seventh
drop, dumping everything the ascent built. No choice of final chord could
fix it, which is why hunting for one felt wrong.

**11. His solution — the Turn.** Rather than flattening the climb, he wrote
a bar and a half of new material:

- Melody steps down A6 → G#6 → F#6 → C#6 → D#6. Lands a third above the loop
  target instead of a seventh above.
- **Bass goes sequential:** G#–F#–E, then A–G#–E, then G#–F#–D#, then
  F#–E–C#. The same three-note figure transposed successively downward. Best
  bass writing in the piece, and it emerged because the melody needed room.
- Chords G#m7 → C#m(add9) — the pivot finally doing the job originally
  imagined for it, at the seam rather than mid-section.
- Bar 19 bass runs D#–C#–B–A# into the loop; A# is the leading tone to B,
  which is where pulse1 lands.

This is a **turnaround** — structurally the same thing his own Overworld
annotations label "C — borrowed + turnaround."
