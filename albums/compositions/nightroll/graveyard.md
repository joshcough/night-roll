# Graveyard — how it's being made

Started 2026-08-20 (a from-bed web session), grown across 08-21. In
progress — 21 bars, **F♯ minor** (written in G minor, transposed down a
semitone whole). Josh, four bars in: "I really feel like I know what I'm
doing now." Josh, at the lead line: "the absolutely most beautiful thing
I have ever written in my entire life."

## Provenance

- Bar 1 borrowed **essentially from "Cries of the Planet" (FF7)** — his
  words, stated plainly: "good artists borrow, great artists steal."
  The rest is his.
- Bars 6–20 were **played on guitar first** as palm-muted power chords,
  then dumped into the bass voice "because that's the instrument I'm
  working on" — only the roots survived. THE BASS IS A SKETCH PAD, not
  a bass line; don't analyze 6–20 as bass writing. The real
  arrangement question (bass doubles guitar vs. independent line) is
  open; he's studying Ellefson-under-Mustaine (Rust in Peace) to rule.

## The intro (bars 1–5, tempo 80) — findings with their paths

All named pre-transposition in G minor; sounding pitches are now a
semitone lower (tonic F♯, dominant C♯, leading tone E♯).

1. **Triangle bars 1–3 outline the tonic triad horizontally** — one
   chord tone per bar at whole-note harmonic rhythm; bar 4 is approach
   motion climbing home. He named the chord and the building-back-up
   unprompted; "horizontal arpeggiation" was the tutor's wording back.
2. **The downbeat plan:** pulse1's beat 1 moves with the bass — held 3
   of 4 bars, broken ONCE deliberately at bar 3: "I played the flat
   five, and it sounds super spooky, and I love it."
3. **Two leading tones aimed at the key's two structural pitches** —
   ♭5→5 into the dominant (bar 3) and 7→1 into the tonic (bar 4 into
   5). Built both BEFORE knowing the term; described the mechanism
   exactly ("pulls us to the g... pulls us to the d") while declining
   to guess the word; then generalized unprompted: a leading tone is
   "a tool of voice leading," like "the backhanded compliment is a
   tool of the passive aggressive." Naming event, not a handed
   discovery. (Glossary: DEMONSTRATED.)
4. The fixed ostinato observation (beats identical in all four bars,
   variation at the edges) was tutor-supplied context — not scored his.

## The seam (bar 5→6) — his ear beat the reasoning three times

Decision first: **boss battle.** Slow intro, hard splice, fast section;
ostinato dies at the door; texture conventional this time ("I don't
think the bass will carry the melody in this song") — explicitly
contrasted with Cool Bmaj, where the inversion WAS the surprise.

Then he built it, and every measured plan lost to audition:

1. **The hang he'd given up on.** "Sadly, I can't figure it out" — the
   tutor agreed silence should win. He solved it anyway, RHYTHMICALLY:
   leading tone held LONGER than the note before it, both voices
   releasing together at 4.5. The failed hypothesis was "wrong note";
   the answer was "right note, wrong length, wrong release." Remember
   this next time a sustain refuses.
2. **The gap: one eighth.** Candidates discussed were 0.75s and 1.5s;
   the ear said 0.375s — HALF the smallest candidate. Silence after a
   leaning climb inflates.
3. **The tempo: planned double, landed triple** at the time (240; the
   whole question later reopened — currently unsettled at 160, ratio
   vs. subdivision framing on the table, his ear's call).

Also his: the rebar (intro moved two beats earlier so the fast section
starts ON a bar — accidentally making G4–D5 a true pickup and putting
the ostinato on the downbeat).

## The fast section (bars 6–21)

- **Riff with a rest on beat 3** — the hole is what keeps hammered
  eighths from sounding mechanical.
- **Bars 8–9 hammer the leading tone for two full bars** — "feels much
  more sinister than leading back into F♯." Duration flips the
  function: brief = pull, held = a place.
- **Bar 13 — his stated favorite.** Four quarter notes, the only
  rhythmic brake, chromatic wrap around the fifth from above and below.
  Unison tried and REJECTED by ear ("washed out" — octave-fused outer
  voices swallow a middle voice); harmony stays: pulse2 mirrors
  pulse1's wrap gesture offset a beat, making consecutive descending
  tritones on beats 2–3.
- **His chord reading, unassisted and verified:** D major ♯4 (14/16/
  18/20) against C♯ major add4 (15/19) — "a driving force in this."
  C♯ major is V with a raised third, and its E♯ is the SAME pitch as
  the sinister pedal — one note wearing two hats.
- **The lead (pulse1 6–12, pulse2 10–13): treat as precious.** Doubled-
  note ascents halving the melody's speed against the riff; voices
  peeling off the chug one at a time; harmony in parallel fourths that
  break to a tritone. Open observation, offered not answered: the
  melody never sounds the tonic — every degree but F♯ over a bass
  hammering F♯.

## The 08-21/22 sessions — the fast section became the piece

- **Tempo history, for the record**: 80/240 → 120 → 160 → **96/192**
  (raised both ends, kept the 2:1 — the problem was 80's absolute
  slowness, not the ratio). Untried lever on file: thin the intro's
  constant eighths instead.
- **CORRECTION: A repeat is development, not repetition.** The earlier
  "nearly literal repeat" read was wrong and Josh pushed back
  correctly: pulse2 stops chugging at bar 10 and crosses ABOVE pulse1
  into harmony — the riff sheds a player into the melody. "That's
  repetition and development put together." (His Megadeth/Friedman
  reference: the harmony enters high. And the fourths came from his
  hands — two strings, same fret.)
- **Bar 10 is the piece's only complete tonic triad**, arriving after
  TWO BARS of hammered leading tone — his find: the borrowed chord and
  the leading tone are one note doing both jobs (bars 8–10 = rootless
  V → V6 → i). The intro only ever spells the tonic horizontally; with
  three monophonic voices, spending all three on one triad is the most
  expensive gesture available, and it happens once. "It's like heaven."
- **The NES constraint is the organizing principle** — his stated
  goal, verified: strictly monophonic everywhere. Every chord is
  implied by voice-leading across three lines; that's WHY the tonic is
  horizontal, why F-minor exists only as coincident lines, why bar 10
  detonates.
- **The melody is a harmony line** — his late realization, and it
  RESOLVED the tonic-avoidance question: a voice harmonizing the root
  doesn't play the root. Layered: pulse2 harmonizes pulse1 in fourths
  while pulse1 harmonizes the triangle in thirds.
- **Bar 11's one-note revision**: final D5 split into two eighths so
  melody and harmony attack together — the tritone (D/G♯) expands to a
  fifth (D/A) with the resolution now ARTICULATED. His summary: "I
  just cut one note in half... and it sounds even better."
- **Actual timings at 96/192**: intro 10.6s, bars 6–13 10.0s, the bar
  10 arrival ~18s in, all 21 bars 30.6s.
- **Reuse plan**: the 6–12 melody is intended as a leitmotif ("I can
  slow it down... it still will retain the original idea") — works
  because the line is almost entirely stepwise, so tempo and
  accompaniment decide the affect. Uematsu discipline applies: bring
  it back once, changed.
- His verdict on 6–13: "those bars feel professional. For real." The
  marker is legibility — every part's job is audible. Cost: ~45
  min/bar, four voices. "Worth every second."

## Process notes

Two hours → twelve seconds of finished music, his own read: "it all
felt really good... it took as long as it had to take." Naming lag as
the live pattern: twice he built a device correctly and lacked the
word — mechanism first, term second, and the generalization he makes
on his own is the real promotion signal.
